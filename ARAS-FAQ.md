# ARAS: Frequently Asked Questions

## What is ARAS?

ARAS stands for American Retirement Account System. It's a plan to replace the retirement part of Social Security with an account you own. You pay the same 12.4% you pay today. The money goes into your personal account instead of the government's trust fund. It grows over your career. When you retire, you draw from it. Whatever is left when you die passes to your heirs.

## How much would I get?

A median-wage worker earning $60,000 a year, retiring at 62, would build an account worth roughly $698,000. That figure assumes a 4.5% average annual real return (inflation-adjusted) during the 40-year accumulation period, reflecting the mandatory glide path that shifts the portfolio toward bonds as the worker ages.

During retirement, the remaining balance continues to earn a return. The withdrawal formula assumes a 3% nominal return (roughly 0.5% real after inflation) during the drawdown period, since the glide path holds at least 60% in bonds by age 70. Under those assumptions, the account would pay roughly $2,160 per month, tax-free, and it's inheritable.

If the drawdown return is higher - say 4.5% nominal, or roughly 2% real - the monthly payment would be roughly $2,500. If it's 5% nominal (roughly 2.5% real), the payment would be roughly $2,950.

The current system pays that same worker about $1,540 per month, partially taxable, with nothing left for heirs. Under ARAS, the account is yours. It pays substantially more. And you can pass it on.

## Does everyone get more?

In general, yes. Low-wage workers get a guaranteed floor that prevents destitution. Median workers get substantially more than the current benefit. High earners build larger accounts because there is no income cap, up to the $5 million lifetime balance cap. The only workers who might get less are those who retire into a severe, prolonged market downturn - and even then, the floor catches them. For the vast majority of workers, ARAS pays more.

## Is this privatization?

It replaces the retirement portion of Social Security with personal accounts. But it keeps disability and survivors benefits pooled, adds a minimum floor, and ensures no one falls into destitution. It's ownership with a safety net, not a free-market free-for-all. Both parts are necessary. The ownership creates wealth and inheritance. The safety net prevents destitution.

## How much do I pay?

The same 12.4% that comes out of your paycheck today. Half comes from you, half from your employer. Nothing changes about the total rate. The only change is where the money goes.

## Where does the money go?

Into your personal account. It's invested in a small number of low-cost index funds. A board of fiduciaries manages the system, not politicians. You can see your balance anytime. Once a year, you can change how it's invested.

## Who oversees the accounts?

The accounts are managed by an independent board, modeled on the federal Thrift Savings Plan (TSP). The TSP is the retirement plan the federal government uses for its own employees. It has been running since 1986. It is managed by a board of fiduciaries who are legally required to act in the account holders' interest, not the government's. The board hires asset managers through competitive bidding. Contracts are rebid every two years. Administrative costs are paid by the account holders, not taxpayers. The board publishes annual audits and cost reports.

The system is not run by politicians. It is not run by Wall Street. It is run by a board with a legal duty to you.

You have secure, 24/7 access to your account through an online portal and mobile app. You can see your balance, your contribution history, and your projected retirement income at any time. Once a year, you can change how your money is invested. You can choose to be more conservative than the glide path requires, but not less. That keeps you protected while giving you control.

## Can I lose money?

Yes. The market goes up and down. That's a real risk. But the system is designed to reduce it. As you get closer to retirement, more of your money moves into bonds. By age 70, at least 60% of your account is in bonds. The withdrawal formula is recalculated every year to keep the account on track to last to age 90. And if your account falls short, the floor catches you. You won't be left with nothing.

## What happens if I die before I retire?

Your account passes to your heirs. That's different from today. Under the current system, if you die before you collect, your family gets nothing. Under ARAS, they inherit your account.

## Who can inherit my account?

Anyone you designate. There is no restriction to children or immediate family. You can name anyone you want as your beneficiary. If you die without naming a beneficiary, the account passes to your spouse, then to your children, then to your estate, in that order. The account is exempt from estate taxes. It is treated as your property, not as a government benefit.

## What happens if I live a long time?

Your account is designed to last to age 90. If you live longer and your account runs low, the floor catches you. You won't be left with nothing.

## What about disability and survivors benefits?

Those stay. They're pooled separately and paid the same way they are today. Nothing changes about how those benefits are calculated or who qualifies.

## What about people who don't earn much?

They keep the full contribution rate in their own account. They're not penalized for earning less. And if their account is too small to provide a decent retirement, the floor tops them up. The floor is set at 28% of the National Average Wage Index. It rises with wages, so it keeps pace with the standard of living.

## What about rich people?

They pay the same total rate, but more of their contribution goes into the pooled programs that fund disability, survivors, and the floor. They can still build a substantial private account - up to $5 million. Once their account hits $5 million, their private account contribution drops to zero and their entire 12.4% goes to the pools. That prevents the system from becoming an unlimited tax shelter. There is no income cap, so high earners still build larger accounts than they could under the current system. But they can't build dynastic wealth through ARAS.

## How does the transition work?

It happens gradually, by age. People 51 and older stay in the current system. People 20 and younger are fully in the new system. Everyone in between is in a mix. The younger you are, the more you're in the new system. No one over 51 is affected at all.

## Does this cost money?

Yes, but only during the transition. The transition costs money because the old system is still paying benefits to current retirees while the new accounts are being built. Most of that cost is concentrated in the first 25 to 30 years. After that, it tapers down. By year 50, the deficit is fully extinguished, and the system becomes a net positive for the federal budget from that point forward.

The transition cost is not spread evenly. It falls heaviest on those most able to pay it - high earners and the ultra-wealthy. During the transition, high earners pay a temporary surcharge above the standard 12.4% rate. That surcharge phases down after year 25 and disappears entirely by year 50. After that, the federal government is permanently freed from funding legacy retirement benefits.

The alternative - doing nothing - is not free either. According to the 2026 Social Security Trustees Report, the current system has a 75-year unfunded obligation of $29.3 trillion in present value. Over the infinite horizon, the permanent structural gap is $71.9 trillion in present value. If nothing is done, benefits get cut automatically by roughly 22% when the trust fund depletes in 2034, or Congress is forced to raise taxes, cut spending, or borrow to cover the gap. Those costs are already scheduled to arrive. ARAS replaces a permanent, growing liability with a temporary, declining one that ends by year 50.

## Will my benefits be cut?

If you're 51 or older, no. You stay in the current system. If you're younger, your benefits are different - but they're also yours. You own them. You can pass them on. And for most workers, the total retirement income is higher than what the current system would pay.

## Why should I trust this?

You shouldn't trust it blindly. Read the framework. Look at the numbers. Ask questions. The framework is designed to be scored, criticized, and revised. It's not a slogan. It's a plan.

## What happens if I outlive my account?

The floor catches you. It's tied to the National Average Wage Index and adjusted for inflation. You won't be left with nothing.

## What happens if the Floor Reserve runs out?

It's backed by the full faith and credit of the United States. If it runs short, Congress has to act. The framework doesn't pretend otherwise.

## What happens if the market crashes right before I retire?

The glide path protects you. By age 70, at least 60% of your account is in bonds. That limits the damage from a stock market crash. The withdrawal formula then adjusts to keep the account on track to last to 90.

## Can I change my investments?

Yes. Once a year, you can change your allocation. You can be more conservative than the glide path requires, but not less. That keeps you protected.

## What about the deficit?

The current system has a $71.9 trillion infinite horizon unfunded obligation. ARAS eliminates it by year 50. Once the transition is complete, the government is permanently freed from funding legacy retirement benefits.

## Why hasn't this been done before?

Because it requires both ownership and a safety net - which means it doesn't fit neatly into either political party's orthodoxy. The left doesn't like private accounts. The right doesn't like floors and progressive contribution tables. ARAS is designed to solve the problem, not to serve an ideology.
